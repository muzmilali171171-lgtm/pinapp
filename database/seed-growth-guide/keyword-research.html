Pinterest's search is more sophisticated than a lot of creators give it credit for — it doesn't just look for exact keyword matches, it evaluates the actual topic and content of a pin. That changes how keyword research should work here compared to traditional SEO, and it's worth understanding the difference before you build a whole content plan around the wrong assumption.

Proving It to Yourself

Run a quick experiment: search a common, high-intent phrase like "breakfast ideas" on Pinterest in a private browsing window and look closely at the top results. It's common to find that several of the best-performing pins never use that exact phrase in their title or description at all — the match is topical, not literal.

This doesn't mean keywords don't matter — it means they function differently than in traditional search SEO. In classic SEO, you target a phrase, build a page around it, and expect to rank for that specific term. On Pinterest, a pin built around "breakfast ideas" might end up ranking primarily for a more specific term buried in the image itself, like "pancake recipe" — because Pinterest is matching the visual and topical content, not just the literal text you wrote.

The Right Strategy: Go Wide, Not Deep

Because Pinterest can surface a pin for related terms you didn't explicitly target, the winning approach is breadth. Cover as many long-tail variations and subtopics within your niche as you reasonably can, rather than pouring all your effort into ranking for one or two flagship keywords. Create a wide spread of content, see what actually gains traction, and then scale up whatever's working — the research in the earlier "Iterate" step of this guide.

Where to Actually Find Keywords

1
Study your competitors first. What are established accounts in your niche already targeting successfully? That's a fast, low-effort starting point.
2
Use Pinterest's own autocomplete. Type a core term into the search bar and note what it suggests before you finish typing — these are genuine, high-frequency search patterns pulled straight from real user behavior.
3
Use the filter pills above search results. After completing a search, Pinterest surfaces a row of related refinement terms — each one is effectively a keyword suggestion worth considering.
4
Check the "more like this" shelf on competitor pins. Opening a well-performing pin surfaces related suggestions — a direct window into what Pinterest associates with that content.
5
Watch what a brand-new account gets suggested. A fresh profile with no history reveals broader category-level interest that a personalized, algorithm-shaped feed might otherwise hide from you.

If you want volume data attached to your keyword list, dedicated third-party Pinterest keyword research tools can add that layer — though the manual research above will get you most of the way there on its own. Early on, favor easier, lower-competition long-tail keywords over the single highest-volume term in your niche, since a new account has a much easier time earning traction on the former. Don't forget the alt text field either — it's a legitimate place for secondary keyword variations.

Once You Have Real Data: Iterate on Your Winners

Once you're seeing meaningful monthly impressions — somewhere in the tens of thousands range is a reasonable point to start — shift from external research to studying your own analytics. This is a step a lot of creators skip, often because they're used to treating Google-style SEO tools as the only trustworthy source and haven't built the habit of trusting their own Pinterest data instead.

Look at what's actually working in your account, then create deliberately more content in that exact direction. If a "best blue bedroom ideas" pin took off, the obvious next moves are blue kitchens, blue living rooms, even more specific angles like blue bedrooms for kids versus blue bedrooms for couples. Keep iterating on subtopics within a proven winner, and Pinterest increasingly comes to trust your account as an authority across that broader topic cluster.

The Habit That Compounds

Keyword research on Pinterest isn't a one-time task you finish before launch — it's an ongoing loop of going wide, watching what resonates, and then going deep on exactly that. Accounts that treat it this way tend to build durable topical authority over time; accounts that pick one keyword and stop researching tend to plateau.
